The Quest Map is a working map of how a physics result travels, from a first idea to a tested result, and where the COSMIC Framework stands on that road.

Each step shows who does the work, what it costs, how often it succeeds and where to go next, with the sources. If you have an idea, start in your lane and follow the footprints. The map is updated with every episode of the Quest.

Where the money comes from

Who: Companies, government, foundations

In 2022 the United States spent about $886 billion on research and development. Businesses paid for 76 percent of it. The federal government paid for 18 percent. Foundations, universities and states paid for the rest.

What that means. Most people who do research for a living are making something: a drug, a chip, a battery, a better product. That is useful work. But it means the questions this site asks, about what the universe is made of at the bottom, are a small corner of the field, paid for mostly by government and a handful of foundations.

Basic research, the kind with no product in view, is where government still leads: about 40 percent of it was federally funded in 2022, down from about 60 percent around 2000. Business now funds about 37 percent.

NSB, Research and Development: U.S. Trends (2024) · NCSES InfoBrief 24-332

The question

Who: A professor (the PI), often with a student

A principal investigator, or PI, is the faculty member or staff scientist who runs a research group and holds its grants. The questions a group works on come from the PI's program, from the last grant, and increasingly from the students' own results.

That is not a criticism. It is why the work is fundable on day one and why it connects to what the field is already arguing about.

The trade. You inherit a running start, and you inherit the direction with it.

Write the proposal

Who: The PI, with postdocs drafting sections

An NSF proposal allows fifteen pages to describe the science, and then needs a budget, a budget justification, a data management plan, biographical sketches, letters from collaborators and a statement of facilities. Writing one well takes weeks to months.

Who actually submits it. Not the scientist. The university's sponsored research office submits it on the institution's behalf, because the award goes to the institution, which then employs the people paid from it.

Who pays. At this step, the federal agencies most physics depends on: the NSF, the Department of Energy's Office of Science (the largest federal sponsor of basic research in the physical sciences, about $8.2 billion in 2024), NASA, and for some work the NIH and the Department of Defense. Private foundations fund a smaller share. Who funds research, and why.

NSF Proposal and Award Policies (PAPPG) · CRS, DOE Office of Science

Panel review: funded?

Who: A panel of outside scientists, then a program officer

Proposals are scored by a panel of scientists from outside the applicant's institution, and a program officer at the agency makes the decision.

  • NSF: about 27 percent of competitive proposals funded in 2024; roughly a quarter for research grants alone.
  • NIH: 13.0 percent of R01 applications funded in 2025.
  • Time: most of a year from submission to money, and most proposals are rewritten and resubmitted at least once.

The part rarely mentioned. A large share never reaches the research. Negotiated overhead rates have commonly run 50 to 65 percent; across NIH-funded institutions the negotiated average was about 58 percent, with roughly 42 percent effectively paid. In May 2025 the NSF capped its rate at 15 percent for universities; a federal court struck the cap down that June. The attempt shows how much was riding on that number.

NSF funding rates · NIH success rates · NSF 15% cap, vacated June 2025

Hire the people

Who: Graduate students, postdocs, technicians, undergraduates

The grant buys people. Most of the experiments, calculations and code behind a physics paper are done by people early in their careers.

  • Graduate students usually start as undergraduates helping in a lab, often through a summer research program, then apply to a PhD program and choose an advisor in their first or second year. Most are paid as research assistants from the advisor's grant, as teaching assistants, or by a fellowship. US physics departments granted about 1,900 PhDs in 2018–19, and the degree took about six years on average.
  • Postdocs have a PhD and work on fixed contracts of two or three years, often several in a row, hoping for a permanent post. There were about 65,850 postdocs in US science, engineering and health in 2023.
  • Technicians, engineers and staff scientists build and run the equipment and keep the data flowing. Their names appear on papers less often than their work does.

What it costs them. In a 2018 survey of 2,279 graduate students in 26 countries, 41 percent scored moderate to severe anxiety and 39 percent moderate to severe depression, against about 6 percent in the general population on the same scales. It was a self-selected sample, so the true rate may be lower, but the gap is large. More on who does the work.

AIP, physics PhDs 2018–19 · NCSES, postdocs 2023 · Evans et al., Nature Biotechnology 36 (2018) 282

Pre-registration (optional)

Who: The group, if it chooses to

Physics rarely pre-registers. The practice grew in medicine and psychology, where it was introduced to stop the analysis being chosen after the result is known, and it is spreading into data-heavy physics.

Why it matters here. Without it, a claim's date is the date it was published, not the date it was thought of. Every argument about priority on this site turns on that distinction.

Center for Open Science, preregistration

Instrument time and the work

Who: Students and postdocs run it; a committee allocates it

Money is not the scarcest resource. Time on the instrument is, and it is allocated by committee to teams with institutions behind them.

The competition. For JWST Cycle 5, 2,639 proposals asked for 101,751 hours against roughly 8,000 available: about thirteen requests for every hour on offer.

Who is at the instrument. Usually a student or postdoc, often overnight, often for weeks. The analysis that follows is theirs too.

STScI, JWST programs

Group review and authorship

Who: The PI and co-authors

Before a paper leaves the group, the co-authors read it and the group decides the author list. In most of physics outside the very large collaborations, the first author is usually the person who did most of the work, often a student or postdoc, and the last author is usually the PI who runs the group. Particle physics collaborations list hundreds or thousands of names alphabetically.

What studies of who did what show. When journals began asking authors to state their contributions, the pattern was clear: first and last authors do most of the work between them, middle authors the least, and as teams grow each author takes on fewer tasks.

Larivière et al., Social Studies of Science 46 (2016) 417 · CRediT contributor roles

The question

Who: Research managers and senior scientists

The subject is chosen where the physics and the roadmap overlap. Google Quantum AI works on error correction because a useful quantum computer cannot exist without it, and genuine physics gets done on the way to a product.

The trade. Enormous resources, aimed only where they pay.

Budget decision

Who: Company management

The decision is internal and can be made in a week. There is no panel, no resubmission, no overhead argument, and no public record of what was declined.

What it buys. Speed, and hardware nobody else has.

What it costs. The funder chooses the question, and the record shows that matters: across 75 studies, industry-funded drug and device research was about twice as likely to report favorable results and about two and a half times as likely to reach favorable conclusions.

Cochrane, industry sponsorship and research outcome

Staff scientists and engineers

Who: PhD scientists, engineers, interns

Industry researchers are employees, hired for their skills, often straight from a PhD or a postdoc. Many come through internships during graduate school.

The difference from a university. The pay is usually better and the contracts longer. In return, employment agreements normally assign inventions made on the job to the employer, and what may be published is the company's decision.

IP and patent clearance

Who: Patent lawyers and the research team

In most countries a public disclosure before filing can destroy the right to a patent, so the paper waits for the lawyers. Applications publish about eighteen months after filing, which is why competitors' filings are readable long before their products ship.

The hard edge. In the United States a patent application can be placed under a secrecy order, and the work disappears from public science entirely.

The work, on company hardware

Who: Staff scientists and engineers

No allocation committee and no queue. Also no obligation to release the raw data, which is the reason some industry results cannot be independently replicated even in principle.

Worked example. The Willow quantum error-correction result was published in Nature in December 2024, on hardware only Google has.

Willow, Nature 2024

Publish, or keep it?

Who: Management and legal

A company publishes when publishing helps it: to recruit, to establish credibility with customers and investors, to set a standard, or once the patents are filed. Otherwise a result can stay a trade secret indefinitely.

What that means for the rest of us. The published record of industry research is a selection, chosen by the company, from a much larger body of work nobody outside will see.

The question

Who: You

Nobody has to approve the question. No roadmap constrains it, no committee scores it, no supervisor has to be persuaded first. This is real, and it is the reason independent work exists at all.

The other side of it. Nobody is assigned to tell you it has been done before, or that it is wrong. Finding that out is now your job.

Write it down

Who: You

A physics idea is read as a paper: a stated claim, the derivation, the references, and what would prove it wrong. The field writes in LaTeX, which is free, and journals provide templates for it.

What it costs. Nothing but time. Get an ORCID iD at this step as well. It is free, it is the identifier journals and funders ask for, and it keeps your work attached to your name.

ORCID · our framework, written in full · every version, dated

Fund it: do you need an institution?

Who: You, or an organisation that will host you

Almost every research grant requires an institution to receive the money. The NSF states that, unless a solicitation says otherwise, unaffiliated individuals are ineligible to receive its funding directly, and it encourages them to affiliate with an organisation that can meet federal grant rules. Academic researchers without an affiliation must speak to a program officer before applying.

Note what kind of wall this is. It is administrative, not intellectual. Nobody has judged the work; the form simply has no box for you.

The ways through.

  • Self-funding, which is what most independents do.
  • A fiscal sponsor: a nonprofit that applies for and holds the grant on your behalf and handles the reporting, for a fee, typically somewhere between 5 and 15 percent.
  • Small foundation grants and crowdfunding, for modest amounts.

What the institute intends. After incorporation, Ic² plans to become an institution independent researchers can affiliate with for their grant applications.

NSF PAPPG, unaffiliated individuals · fiscal sponsorship, how it works

Public, dated deposit

Who: You, and a repository such as Zenodo

With no institution to vouch for you, a dated public deposit is the only thing separating a prediction from a story told afterward. Zenodo, run by CERN, issues a permanent DOI and a date that cannot be edited later. It is free.

What has to be in it. A number, an uncertainty, a named instrument that could contradict it, and the outcome you will accept either way.

Where we are. Three missions are registered and waiting.

Zenodo · our registered missions

Find readers and collaborators

Who: Other independents, retired academics, students, volunteers

In a university the first readers come with the job: co-authors, the group, the seminar. An independent researcher starts with none. The people who end up helping are usually other independents, retired academics with time to read, students, and specialists who answer an email because the question is precise.

What works. Write to authors whose work you build on, with one specific question. Show the derivation so someone can check it in an afternoon. Thank and credit everyone who helps, by name.

Why this step matters later. The endorser arXiv requires, the colleague who checks the units, and the first person to cite you will all come from here.

Test against public data

Who: You, using data the large collaborations release

No instrument time, so the strategy is to predict something a major survey is going to measure anyway, and then accept the answer.

This only works because the large collaborations publish. DESI, Rubin and the Simons Observatory all release data to anyone, which is the single policy that makes independent cosmology possible.

Where we are. First deciding data expected in 2027.

Internal check

Who: Co-authors, a review board, or whoever you can persuade to read it

Before anything goes out, someone checks it: a co-author, a review board, or in the independent case only you and whoever you can persuade to read it.

This is where the lanes stop being equal. Industry has a formal technical review. A university group has co-authors and a seminar. An independent researcher has no one by default, which is exactly the gap an adversarial pre-check is meant to fill: do the units balance, does the derivation close, has this already been refuted, could the prediction be proven wrong.

arXiv: are you endorsed?

Who: An established arXiv author; about 300 volunteer moderators

arXiv was launched on 14 August 1991 by Paul Ginsparg. It is free to read and free to post, and it passed two million papers by the end of 2021. Physics runs on it: most papers appear here before, or instead of, a journal.

Who may post. A new author must be endorsed by an established author in that subject area. About 300 volunteer moderators screen submissions; roughly 6 percent are held and about 2 percent rejected.

Changed January 2026. arXiv no longer accepts an institutional email address as the qualifier for endorsement, after a rise in non-scientific submissions. Affiliation alone will not open this door any more; only a person who reads the work will.

Why the wall exists. arXiv is run by a small staff and volunteers, and it is flooded with submissions. Endorsement is how it keeps moderation possible. For an independent author the way through is a reader from the previous step.

arXiv endorsement · the 2026 change

Post the preprint

Who: You

Posting is free. The paper appears in the next daily announcement after moderation, and from then on it carries a public date and a permanent identifier.

What it is not. A preprint has been screened, not reviewed. It establishes when you said something, not that it is right.

Why most physicists post before submitting. It gets the work read months before a journal would publish it, and readers' comments often improve it before review.

Choose a journal

Who: You and your co-authors

Fit matters most. Most rejections happen because a paper does not suit the journal, so read a few recent issues first. In foundations and cosmology, for example: the Physical Review family (American Physical Society), Classical and Quantum Gravity (IOP), Foundations of Physics (Springer).

Then cost. Many society journals publish free on the subscription route and charge only for open access. Gold open-access journals charge every author.

Avoid predatory journals. If a journal emails you first, promises fast acceptance and asks for a fee, it is selling publication, not review. A paper there can count against you.

Submit

Who: You

How an independent researcher does it.

  • Format the paper with the journal's template (for APS journals, REVTeX).
  • Give your affiliation honestly as Independent researcher, with a postal address and your ORCID iD.
  • Write a one-page cover letter: what the paper claims, why it suits this journal, and that it is not under review elsewhere.
  • Suggest qualified reviewers who are not your collaborators, and name any you want excluded, with a reason.
  • Declare funding (self-funded is a valid answer) and any competing interests.

What it costs. Submission to most physics journals is free. Payment comes at acceptance, and only for open access: at APS in 2025, $2,840 for Physical Review A to E and $3,980 for Physical Review Letters. The subscription route costs nothing. Most high-energy physics articles are covered by the SCOAP3 consortium.

APS publication charges

The editor's desk

Who: One editor

An editor reads the submission first and desk-rejects most of what arrives before any reviewer sees it, usually for fit, sometimes for presentation, and sometimes because the claim looks too large for the evidence.

Who the editors are. Working scientists, often part-time, with their own interests. A 2017 study of 713 editors at 52 influential US medical journals found 51 percent had received general payments from industry in 2014.

Liu et al., BMJ 359 (2017) j4619

Peer review

Who: Two or three reviewers, usually anonymous

Peer review is two or three unpaid specialists, chosen by an editor, usually from the same small field as the author, sometimes competitors for the same grants. It has no investigators, no subpoena power and no budget. It runs on the assumption that a reviewer's interests are disclosed.

Who those people are. A 2024 study of 1,962 physician peer reviewers at the BMJ, NEJM, JAMA and the Lancet found more than half had received industry payments between 2020 and 2022, totalling 1.06 billion dollars.

What it does not catch. Both of physics's famous fabrication cases passed peer review repeatedly at the most selective journals. What caught them was replications that could not reproduce the results, and independent researchers reading the figures.

JAMA (2024), payments to peer reviewers

The decision

Who: The editor, on the reviewers' advice

Most submissions to a selective journal are rejected. An address that reads Independent starts further back: the 1982 Peters and Ceci experiment resubmitted already-published papers under unknown names and affiliations, and most were rejected.

Measured version. In a randomized trial at a 2017 conference, reviewers who could see author identities were significantly more likely to recommend acceptance for famous authors (odds multiplier 1.63), top universities (1.58) and top companies (2.10), on the same papers.

Tomkins, Zhang and Heavlin, PNAS 114 (2017) 12708

Revise and resubmit

Who: You, answering the reviewers

Answer every reviewer point in a reply letter, say what you changed and where, and argue politely where you disagree. Two or three rounds is ordinary, and a year can pass.

Why the loop matters to us. Every pass through it is time in which the dated public record is the only thing establishing when the claim was made.

Accepted: publication and the bill

Who: The publisher

Acceptance is not the end. Open access is paid by the author: about 12,850 US dollars at Nature, 7,350 at Nature Communications, 2,850 at Scientific Reports, and commonly 2,000 to 6,000 elsewhere.

Who feels it. A university pays from a grant or a library deal, so the author never sees the invoice. A company pays without noticing. An independent researcher pays personally, chooses the free subscription route, or asks for a waiver.

And then it is paid for twice. Libraries subscribe as well. RELX, Elsevier's parent, reported about 1.17 billion pounds of adjusted operating profit on 3.05 billion of revenue in its scientific division, a margin near 38 percent. Authors and peer reviewers are paid nothing.

Nature charges

What publishing gets you

Who: The field

  • A version of record: the final, citable text, preserved by the publisher and by archives even if the journal closes.
  • A documented review: proof that specialists read it and did not find it wrong.
  • Indexing: the paper enters the databases researchers search and that citations are counted in.
  • Currency: hiring committees, promotion panels and grant panels count peer-reviewed papers. For an independent author it is the strongest answer to "who checked this?"

What it does not get you. It does not make the result true, and it does not guarantee anyone reads it.

Cited, or not

Who: Everyone who reads, or does not read, the paper

A citation is a pointer to the work a paper builds on. Two well-documented effects shape who gets them.

The Matthew effect. Robert Merton showed in 1968 that well-known scientists receive more credit than lesser-known colleagues for comparable work. A famous co-author's name draws the citations.

Copied citations. By tracing how misprints in references spread, Simkin and Roychowdhury estimated that only about one citer in five had read the paper they cited; the rest copied the reference from another paper's list. It is a model-based estimate, but it explains why a few papers collect most of the citations.

Merton, Science 159 (1968) 56 · Simkin and Roychowdhury, Complex Systems 14 (2003)

Replication and the sigma bar

Who: Other groups, other instruments

A result counts when someone else can reproduce it, and when the evidence clears the field's bar. Particle physics sets its bar explicitly, which most fields do not.

  • 3 sigma is called evidence: roughly a 1 in 740 chance of arising from noise.
  • 5 sigma is called discovery: roughly 1 in 3.5 million. The Higgs boson was announced in July 2012 at 5 sigma.

Where our own tests sit. DESI's evolving dark energy runs about 2.8 to 4.2 sigma depending on which supernova compilation is combined. That is evidence, and it is not discovery, and saying so plainly is the whole discipline.

CERN on the Higgs · our record

Addendum or further testing

Who: The original authors, and anyone else

A result below the bar is routed back: more data, a tighter analysis, or a narrower claim. Most results in physics live here for years.

The honest risk. This is also where a result can be quietly reshaped until it agrees with the data. The protection is the same one this institute relies on everywhere: the prediction was registered, with a date, before the data existed.

Accepted

The result enters the field: replicated, cited and built on, until something better replaces it. That is the ending everyone means by a result.

Modified

Most ideas that survive do so changed. A narrower claim, a corrected number, a different mechanism. This is a result too, and it is the most common good one.

Set aside

A result that cannot find a venue is not necessarily wrong, and it is not necessarily right either. It stops being part of the conversation, which in practice is the same as not existing. Industry results kept as trade secrets end here too.

Where a public deposit exists, the record survives even when the paper does not. A clear negative result is also a result: it tells everyone where not to look.

Who actually does the research

The name on a grant is one person. The work is done by a team, and most of the team is early in their careers.

Graduate students. Most begin as undergraduates helping in a lab, often through a summer research program, which is how they find out whether they like the work and get the letters of recommendation a PhD application needs. In the first year or two of a PhD they choose an advisor, and from then on they are usually paid as research assistants from that advisor's grant, as teaching assistants, or by a fellowship such as the NSF Graduate Research Fellowship, which pays a $37,000 stipend for three years. US physics departments granted about 1,900 PhDs in 2018–19. The degree took about six years on average.

Not everyone finishes. The Council of Graduate Schools followed students who began PhDs in the early 1990s: in mathematics and the physical sciences about 55 percent had finished within ten years, and most of those who left did so by their third year.

What it costs them. In a 2018 survey of 2,279 graduate students in 26 countries, 41 percent scored moderate to severe anxiety and 39 percent moderate to severe depression, against about 6 percent in the general population measured with the same scales. In Nature's 2019 survey of 6,300 PhD students, 36 percent said they had sought help for anxiety or depression caused by their studies. Both samples chose to respond, so the true rates may be lower, but no reading of them is reassuring.

Postdocs hold a PhD and work on contracts of two or three years, often several in a row, hoping for one of the few permanent posts. There were about 65,850 in US science, engineering and health in 2023.

Everyone else. Technicians, engineers and staff scientists who build and run the instruments. Undergraduates on summer projects. And volunteers: citizen-science projects have had members of the public classify galaxies and search telescope data, and some have been co-authors on the results.

Why this matters to the Quest. These people are the system. Any honest account of how an idea meets the world has to include who carries it.

AIP · Evans et al. 2018 · Nature PhD survey 2019 · NCSES 2023 · Council of Graduate Schools, Ph.D. Completion Project

Is most cited work really someone else's?

It is something people say, and it is partly true, in three different senses.

1. The hands-on work is mostly done by juniors. True for most of physics outside the giant collaborations. The experiments, code and calculations are usually done by graduate students and postdocs. They are normally listed as authors, often first, so the paper itself is not hiding them. Studies of the contribution statements journals now require show first and last authors doing most of the work between them, and middle authors the least.

2. The recognition goes up the ladder. True, and it has a name. Robert Merton called it the Matthew effect in 1968: well-known scientists receive more credit than lesser-known colleagues for comparable work. Talks, prizes and press tend to name the head of the lab. A paper becomes known as the PI's paper.

3. Many citations are copied, not read. Probably true. By tracing how misprints in references spread from paper to paper, Simkin and Roychowdhury estimated that only about one citer in five had read the work they cited. It is a model-based estimate, not a survey, but it fits the way a small number of papers collect most citations.

What is not true. A citation is not taking someone's work. It is the opposite: the formal way of saying whose work you are building on. The problem is the credit around it, not the citation itself.

Why it happens. Careers are measured in papers and citations, so the incentives reward volume and visibility. Teams have grown, so individual roles are hidden. And reading every cited paper takes time nobody is paid for.

What we do. Every contributor to the institute's work is named with what they did, using the CRediT contributor roles, and contributors keep ownership of their work.

Merton 1968 · Simkin and Roychowdhury 2003 · Larivière et al. 2016 · CRediT

Who funds research, and why

Businesses: about three quarters of all US research money ($673 billion of $886 billion in 2022). They fund research because it leads to products. That is most of the research done in the country, and most of the people doing it.

Government: about 18 percent overall, and about 40 percent of basic research. The reasons are national security, economic competitiveness and health, and the modern system dates from Vannevar Bush's 1945 report to the President, Science, the Endless Frontier, which argued that basic research pays the nation back in ways no company will fund. For physics the main federal funders are the Department of Energy's Office of Science (the largest federal sponsor of basic research in the physical sciences, about $8.2 billion in 2024), the NSF, NASA, and parts of the Department of Defense.

Private foundations: small in total, important at the edges. The Simons Foundation, the Gordon and Betty Moore Foundation, the John Templeton Foundation, the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation, the Kavli Foundation and others fund questions government panels find too risky or too unusual. They give because a founder cares about a field, for legacy, and because the law requires it: a US private foundation must pay out about 5 percent of its assets every year, or pay a tax on the shortfall.

Universities also spend their own money, mostly from endowments, tuition and state budgets.

Why the money goes to institutions, not people. Public money must be accounted for under federal grant rules, so it is paid to an organisation that can keep the books, run the audits and employ the staff. That is the reason an independent researcher cannot simply apply, and why the institute plans to become an organisation independents can affiliate with.

NSB 2024 · NCSES 24-332 · Bush, 1945 · CRS on DOE Office of Science · 26 U.S.C. § 4942

How an independent submits to a journal, and what it costs

  1. Get an ORCID iD. Free, and asked for by most journals.
  2. Post the preprint if you can (see the arXiv step on the map). If you cannot yet, deposit it on Zenodo for a dated record.
  3. Choose the journal by fit: read recent issues and the journal's scope statement.
  4. Format with the journal's template. APS journals use REVTeX; most others provide LaTeX and Word templates.
  5. Affiliation: write Independent researcher, with a postal address. Do not invent an institution.
  6. Cover letter: one page. The claim, why this journal, and a statement that it is not under review elsewhere.
  7. Reviewers: suggest three to five qualified people who are not your collaborators. You may ask that named people be excluded, with a reason.
  8. Declarations: funding (self-funded is a valid answer), competing interests, data availability.
  9. Submit through the journal's online system, then wait: weeks for a desk decision, months for review.

What it costs. Submitting is free at most physics journals. On the subscription route, publishing is free too. Open access is charged on acceptance: at APS in 2025, $2,840 for Physical Review A to E and $3,980 for Physical Review Letters; about 12,850 US dollars at Nature. Most high-energy physics articles are covered by the SCOAP3 consortium. Some journals waive charges on request; ask before you submit, not after.

Warning signs. A journal that invites you by email, promises acceptance in days, or asks for a fee before review is not a journal you want.

APS charges · Nature charges · ORCID

What is gained by publishing

For the work. A version of record that will not change, a permanent DOI, preservation in archives that outlive the journal, and a place in the databases researchers search and citations are counted in.

For the claim. A documented review: two or three specialists read it and did not find it wrong. That is less than proof, and more than nothing.

For the person. Peer-reviewed papers are the currency of science. Hiring, promotion and grant panels count them, and for someone without an institution a published paper is the strongest answer to the question "who checked this?"

What a preprint already gives you. A public date and readers. Many physicists are read on arXiv long before, or without, a journal.

What publishing does not give you. Truth, and attention. Most papers are cited rarely. A result becomes knowledge only when others replicate it and build on it.

The five paths

Where an Idea Can Go

An idea does not become knowledge by being right. It becomes knowledge by passing through one of these paths, each with its own gatekeepers, costs and timescales. Open any path to see what it actually asks of you. The Quest reports where the COSMIC Framework stands on each.

The clearest case is the tobacco industry. It funded scientists, published papers and convened committees, and its goal was never to find out whether smoking caused cancer. Its own documents, released in litigation, describe the aim: manufacture doubt. That is a search for profit wearing the clothes of a search for truth, and it is why the question "what is this path paid to conclude?" comes before any argument about the data.

InstitutionalUniversities, public research, journalsOpen

The steps. Get an affiliation, usually a doctorate and a position. Win funding: in the United States in 2025, NIH funded 13.0% of R01 applications and the National Science Foundation about a quarter of its proposals. Get time on an instrument: for JWST Cycle 5, 2,639 proposals asked for 101,751 hours against 8,000 available, about 13 requests for every hour. Do the work, write it up, and send it to a journal, where two or three unpaid reviewers, usually from the same small field, advise an editor. Revise. Publish. Then wait for someone to replicate it.

The gates. Affiliation, funding, instrument time, reviewers, and a field's willingness to spend attention on you.

The clock. Five to fifteen years from idea to accepted result is normal. Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912; geology accepted it in the 1960s.

The people in it. This path runs on graduate students and postdocs. Our blog post What Most People Think Science Is goes through what that costs them: the anxiety and depression rates, the stipends, and how few ever reach a permanent position. They are players in the Quest as much as any instrument.

Where we are. Framework papers and the pre-registrations are public on Zenodo. No journal submission yet.

BusinessIndustry partners, private funding, patentsOpen

The steps. Protect first, publish second: in most countries a public disclosure before filing can end your right to a patent. Sign non-disclosure agreements. Find an investor who accepts a return in three to seven years, which is faster than most physics pays out. Agree who owns the result and who may publish it.

The gates. Money, intellectual property, and a market. A funder decides which questions get asked.

The catch. Industry-funded work is more likely to reach conclusions the funder prefers. A Cochrane review of 75 studies covering 4,583 trials found industry-sponsored drug and device research significantly more likely to report favorable results and favorable conclusions. Not fraud, usually: the influence sits in which question is asked, how the study is designed, and what gets reported.

Where we are. Nothing to report yet.

MilitaryDefense programs and classified workOpen

The steps. Answer a defense solicitation, such as a DARPA broad agency announcement. Clear the security requirements. Accept that results may be classified, and that export-control rules can limit who you may even talk to. In the United States, a patent application can be placed under a secrecy order.

The gates. Clearance, classification, and a mission case.

The catch. This path can fund work nothing else will fund, and it can also make the work disappear. Classified results cannot be tested in public, which is the opposite of what this institute is for.

Where we are. Not pursued.

IndependentIndependent work, public data, open recordsOpen

The steps. Write the idea down in public with a date. Deposit it where the record cannot be edited later. Use public data, because you will not be allocated an instrument. Derive something specific enough to be proven wrong. Then ask people who can check it to check it.

The gates, honestly. Most grants require an institution. Instrument time requires a proposal team. arXiv asks new authors for an endorsement from an established author before they may post in a field. Journals desk-reject most of what arrives, and an author whose affiliation reads Independent starts further back. Conferences cost money and usually want a host.

Why the door is heavy. Most independent claims are wrong, so the filters are mostly doing their job. That is the uncomfortable part: the same filter that stops nonsense also stops a good idea with no credentials behind it. Faraday, Einstein in the patent office, Ramanujan writing to Hardy and Wegener all got through anyway, and each of them still had to convince the field on its terms.

What actually works. Say what would prove you wrong, before the data arrives. Show the derivation so someone can check it in an afternoon. Publish every result. That is the whole strategy here, and it is the only one available without a badge.

Where we are. This is our path: an independent institute testing against public data, with predictions registered in advance.

InternationalWorldwide collaborations and shared instrumentsOpen

The steps. Large instruments belong to consortia of nations: CERN, the European Space Agency, ITER, the big survey collaborations. Work enters through a member institution, passes internal review, and is released on the collaboration's schedule. Author lists run to hundreds or thousands of names.

The whole map. Not all of it is in Europe and North America. China's JUNO neutrino observatory, running since August 2025, published its first result in Nature in June 2026. South Africa's MeerKAT will become part of the Square Kilometre Array. SESAME in Jordan is a synchrotron shared by Cyprus, Egypt, Iran, Israel, Jordan, Pakistan, Palestine and Turkey. And the Rubin Observatory that will decide our test COSMIC-007 sits on a mountain in Chile. The Global Research page lists where each region publishes and how to reach it.

The gates. Membership, national funding cycles, and a collaboration's own priorities. Decisions are made years ahead: the instruments that will test our predictions were designed and funded long before the predictions existed.

The opening. These collaborations publish their data. Anyone can test a prediction against it, which is how an independent researcher can use the largest instruments ever built without being a member of the club.

Where we are. Our tests rely on public releases from DESI, the Simons Observatory and Rubin.

The machinery every path runs through

The five paths above are drawn apart for clarity, but they are not separate worlds. They share the same three systems, and most of the crossings between paths happen inside them. An idea does not move without money, a dated record, and a stamp.

The money

Why it matters. Nothing happens without it, and almost all of it requires an institution to receive it. This is the single hardest wall for an independent researcher, and it is administrative rather than intellectual.

The odds. The NSF funded about 27 percent of competitive proposals in 2024, and about a quarter of research grants. The NIH funded 13.0 percent of R01 applications in 2025. A cycle from submission to money runs the better part of a year, and most proposals are rewritten and resubmitted.

What applying costs, before anyone wins anything. Preparing and reviewing applications is itself expensive, and the bill lands on the applicant. A 2026 study of four UK schemes at the Medical Research Council and the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council put the transaction cost of the process at 7 to 13 percent of the value of the grant, with 89 percent of that cost borne by the applicants, 7 percent by reviewers and 4 percent by panel members. An institution absorbs that cost inside salaries already being paid. A researcher without one pays it in their own time, and pays it again on every resubmission. Pollitt et al., F1000Research 15:534 (2026). Read it with its limits in view: two funders in one country, an 11 percent response rate from 12,617 people surveyed, and a version with one approving review and one approving with reservations.

The part nobody mentions. A large share of a grant never reaches the work. Universities negotiate overhead rates, for buildings and administration, that have commonly run between 50 and 65 percent; across NIH-funded institutions the negotiated average was about 58 percent, with about 42 percent effectively paid. In May 2025 the NSF capped its rate at 15 percent for universities, which tells you how much was at stake in that number.

The record

Why it matters. A preprint server timestamps a claim in public before any journal has ruled on it. For an independent researcher it is the whole game: it is the only way to establish that you said something before the data arrived, which is the difference between a prediction and a story told afterward.

arXiv. Launched on 14 August 1991 by the physicist Paul Ginsparg, free to read and to post, past two million papers by the end of 2021. Physics and astronomy effectively run on it: most papers appear there before, or instead of, a journal.

The catch. It is not open to everyone. A new author needs an endorsement from an established one before posting in a field. About 300 volunteer moderators screen submissions; roughly 6 percent are put on hold and about 2 percent rejected. A preprint is also not peer reviewed, and saying so plainly is part of using one honestly.

Where we are. Our predictions are deposited on Zenodo, run by CERN, which issues a permanent DOI and a date that cannot be edited afterward.

The stamp

Why journals still matter. Not for distribution: the internet solved that. They matter because a journal supplies the version of record, a documented review, and the currency that hiring committees, promotion panels and funders actually count. A result outside a journal has not been rejected. It has simply not been priced.

What it costs to publish. Open access is paid by the author. Publishing open access in Nature costs about 12,850 US dollars per paper. Nature Communications is about 7,350, Scientific Reports about 2,850. Most researchers pay from a grant, which means the funding wall and the publishing wall are the same wall.

What it costs everyone else. Libraries pay again through subscriptions. RELX, Elsevier's parent, reported about 1.17 billion pounds of adjusted operating profit on 3.05 billion of revenue in its scientific division, a margin near 38 percent, higher than Apple or Microsoft. The authors are not paid, and neither are the peer reviewers.

Where the paths cross

Most real careers and most real results move between paths rather than staying on one:

  • University to business. A result is patented and licensed through a technology transfer office; the researcher may hold equity in the company that sells it.
  • Business to university. A company funds a laboratory, which shapes which question gets asked long before any data is collected.
  • University to defense. A program is funded by a military agency and can be classified, which removes it from public testing entirely.
  • Independent to affiliated. An independent result travels only when a credentialed group picks it up and repeats it. That is the bridge, and it is the one an independent researcher cannot build alone.
  • International to everyone. The large collaborations release their data publicly, and every other path, including ours, tests against it.

This is why the question is never simply which path an idea is on. It is which path is paying, which path is reviewing, and whether the record of what was claimed survived the journey between them.

Much of the map is not in English

Every path above has the same blind spot. A literature search run in English, through the usual databases, finds research published in North America and Europe and misses much of the rest: Chinese journals and theses, African journals the big indexes barely cover, Latin America's open platforms, Russian-language work. We counted our own book's references in October 2026. Of 368 cited works, 96.5 percent were published in North America or Western Europe. That is a fact about where we looked, not about where the physics is being done.

Where you live can be a key. Many researchers live outside their home country or travel for work, and access follows the library card, not the passport. A database restricted in one country can be open through a university in another, or through a home institution you still belong to. If you have lawful access to something on our Global Research page marked limited, tell us. We never ask for a shared login or anything that breaks a licence; telling us a database is reachable, or that a paper exists, is enough to put it on the map.

Who reviews whom

Peer review is done by two or three unpaid specialists, chosen by an editor, usually from the same small field as the author. They may be competitors for the same grants. They declare conflicts of interest, and journals rely on that declaration.

Where an industry funds the research, the record is documented: the Cochrane review cited above found industry-sponsored studies more likely to report favorable results. The tobacco industry's own documents, released through litigation, show the strategy stated plainly in a 1969 internal memo: doubt is the product, because doubt is what competes with the body of fact in the public mind. This is not an argument against peer review. It is the reason a public, dated record matters more than any assurance, including ours.

Where Else to Look

This map exists because we could not find one. We looked again on 24 September 2026 and still found nothing that puts the paths, the gates and the costs in one place; what continuity there is tends to be written for a classroom. These three are worth your time, and each does one part of it properly. Here is where each one stops.

Peer Review: The Nuts and Bolts

Sense about Science · free PDF

Written by early career researchers for early career researchers, to open up the one gate everybody has heard of and almost nobody has seen working. If you read one thing before sending a paper anywhere, read this.

Where it stops. One gate, covered well. It does not tell you how to reach it without an affiliation, or what it costs once you are through.

OpenAPC

Bielefeld University Library · open data

What institutions actually paid, article by article, rather than what publishers advertise. Universities and funders submit their real invoices; the whole set is downloadable under an open database license, with a treemap for reading it without a spreadsheet.

Where it stops. Open-access charges and transformative agreements only. Submission fees, page charges and color charges are not in it, and neither is anything an unaffiliated author would pay personally.

NCIS and the Ronin Institute

Membership bodies for unaffiliated scholars

Not guides but doors. Both exist to give an independent researcher the affiliation that forms keep asking for. NCIS is vetted, runs six grants and prizes, publishes its own open-access peer-reviewed journal, and its membership is accepted as an affiliation by publishers. The Ronin Institute is a US nonprofit doing the same job.

Where it stops. They solve the affiliation box. They do not tell you which path to take or what waits on it.
