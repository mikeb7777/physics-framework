In October 2025 we announced four confirmations. We had dated each result by its journal, not its first release, and no record of our predictions survives from before the data. None of the four counts. Here is how that happened and what it taught us.

The main storyHow we got here, and the four confirmations that weren’t

Why it matters. A prediction is worth only as much as the record that dates it. This is the story of four results that were called confirmations without one.

On 28 October 2025 the Institute published a release headed “The COSMIC Framework Achieves Perfect Validation Score Across Four Independent Breakthrough Discoveries”. Three more releases the same day said particular predictions had been validated or confirmed.

In September 2026 the Institute audited every earlier claim that a prediction had come before its result. None of the dated records those releases relied on survives from before the results. So none of the four counts as a prediction made in advance. They are now listed in the Results Registry as consistent with the framework and recorded after the data, and the original releases are kept, struck through, on the Superseded Claims page rather than deleted.

This account is drafted in your voice from what you told us and from the site’s own records. Check every line, change anything that is not how it happened, and fill in the brackets.

In my own words. Early in 2024 I did what I thought you were supposed to do. I learned LaTeX, wrote the work up as a paper, and set out to submit it.

That is where I found out how the road is built. To post on arXiv, the physics preprint server, a new author needs an endorsement from someone who already publishes there. Getting one means writing to people you have never met and asking them to vouch for you, and often there is no way even to reach the people you are supposed to ask. Almost nobody answered, and nobody endorsed. Without an endorsement there is no arXiv, and other posting sites route an independent author away too.

Journals were not much different. The Royal Astronomical Society turned the work down because it was not astronomy. That was at least a real answer; most of the time there was none.

So I wrote the book. It was not the plan. It was the only way I could see to put the whole idea in front of people and get a proper no from someone. The first version is dated 5 March 2024, but no file of that version survives, and that matters for what came next.

When results started to arrive, among them DESI’s dark energy measurements in April 2024 and Google’s Willow chip in December 2024, we checked them against the book. To decide whether a prediction had come first, we dated each result by when it appeared in a journal, or when we read it, instead of when it was first released. Measured that way, the predictions looked earlier than the results. By October 2025 we were calling four of them confirmations. I did not see the problem at the time, and neither did Claude, the AI assistant I was working with.

The audit in September 2026 asked a simpler question: what is the oldest record of this prediction that anyone can check? For each of the four, that record came after the result. So none of them counts as a prediction. They are consistent with the framework, and that is all they are.

How it felt to take the four back, and why you chose to do it in public. Two or three sentences.

What others on this road need to know.

  1. Date it before the data, somewhere you cannot edit, and keep every version. A prediction counts only if a dated, permanent record shows it existed first. A Zenodo or OSF deposit with a DOI does that; a website, a draft or a memory does not. Our first version may well have said what we remember, but with no surviving file, nobody can check it.
  2. Date a result by its first public release. Not the journal date, and not the day you read it. Results usually appear months earlier as a preprint, a conference talk or a press release, and that first date is the one a prediction has to beat.
  3. A match found afterwards is consistency, not confirmation. It is still worth recording, under its real name.
  4. “Consistent with” is not “distinguishes”. If the standard theory predicts the same result, the result cannot tell the two apart. Ask what else would have predicted it.
  5. Expect the endorsement wall, and go around it. arXiv needs an endorser and journals keep to their scope, so an independent can be turned away before anyone reads the work. Zenodo needs no endorsement. A dated deposit there is the step we should have taken first, and it would have settled the dates on its own.
  6. Withdraw in the open. Keep the old claim visible, struck through, with what was found. A quiet deletion costs more trust than the mistake did.

What we learnedThe running list

  1. A prediction is only as old as its oldest record anyone can check.
  2. Being consistent with a result is not the same as having predicted it.
  3. A claim withdrawn in public stays part of the record, and that is the point.
  4. A result’s date is its first release, not its journal date.
  5. For an independent, the front doors are mostly shut. A dated public deposit does not need anyone’s permission.

2 · What the data says

At this point on the trail

This footprint sits between Self-funding and Falsifiability and public deposit on the independent lane: the point where an idea either gets a dated public record or does not.

  • Physics rarely pre-registers. The practice grew in medicine and psychology to stop the analysis being chosen after the result is known, and it is spreading into data-heavy physics. Without it, a claim’s date is the date it was published, not the date it was thought of. (Center for Open Science)
  • Funding needs an institution. Almost every research grant requires an institution to receive the money, and many require the lead researcher to hold a faculty-level post. FQxI, for example, accepts independent researchers only as co-investigators.

4 · Options

If you are standing here

  • Register before the data. Deposit the claim, the measure and the decision rule with a permanent DOI before the data arrives: Zenodo or OSF Registries.
  • Have the method reviewed first. Some journals accept Registered Reports, where the method is accepted before the results exist.
  • Find a home for the money. Bodies that accept independent scholars, such as the NCIS or the Ronin Institute, or a fiscal sponsor.
  • Facing the endorsement wall. Read how arXiv endorsement works, ask someone who has published on your exact topic, and send them one short paper rather than a whole theory. Meanwhile, a Zenodo deposit needs no endorser.
  • Check a journal’s scope before submitting. A paper outside the scope is returned without review, which costs months and teaches nothing.
  • If you have already claimed too much, withdraw it openly and keep the record. It is what this issue is about.
