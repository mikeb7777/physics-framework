## A. Proposed, no new research needed

### A1. Who first described the Fibonacci sequence

**Where.** "The Binary Condition and Conservation", p. 28, the first place the
book names the sequence. Element 14 (p. 292) uses the name again and needs no
change once it has been introduced properly.

**Now.**
> This is the Fibonacci sequence: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13.

**Change to.**
> This is the Fibonacci sequence: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. It carries the name of
> Leonardo of Pisa, who brought it to Europe in 1202, but Indian scholars of
> poetic meter had described it centuries earlier, from Pingala to Virahanka
> and Hemachandra [n].

**New reference.**
> Singh, P. (1985). "The so-called Fibonacci numbers in ancient and medieval
> India." *Historia Mathematica*, 12(3), 229–244.

**Why.** The sequence arose in India from counting rhythms, which is the same
kind of origin the book is arguing for: a pattern that falls out of a simple
rule, found independently wherever someone counts carefully.

### A2. The history of π

**Where.** Element 1, p. 51, which cites only reference [13], Archimedes.

**Now.**
> Pi doesn’t describe a property of circles; it emerges from the relationship
> between a circle’s circumference and its diameter [13].

**Change to.**
> Pi doesn’t describe a property of circles; it emerges from the relationship
> between a circle’s circumference and its diameter [13]. That relationship
> was pursued independently across the ancient and medieval world. Egyptian
> scribes worked with a value close to 3.16 in a text first written about
> 1850 BCE. Archimedes bounded
> it with polygons. Liu Hui and Zu Chongzhi in China carried the polygon method
> to seven decimal places by the fifth century. Madhava in Kerala found an
> infinite series for it around 1400, and in 1424 al-Kashi in Samarkand
> computed it to sixteen decimal places, a record that stood for nearly two
> centuries [n, n+1, n+2, n+3].

**New references.**
> Imhausen, A. (2016). *Mathematics in Ancient Egypt: A Contextual History*.
> Princeton University Press.
>
> Martzloff, J.-C. (1997). *A History of Chinese Mathematics*. Springer.
>
> Plofker, K. (2009). *Mathematics in India*. Princeton University Press.
>
> Berggren, J. L. (2016). *Episodes in the Mathematics of Medieval Islam*, 2nd
> ed. Springer.

**Why.** It strengthens the book's own point. A constant that five
civilizations reached separately, by different methods, is better evidence
that π belongs to the geometry and not to a culture.

### A3. The oldest written binary arithmetic

**Where.** "The Binary Condition and Conservation", p. 27, after "Every
binary in physics traces back to this original twoness."

**Add.**
> People found that twoness long before physics did. Egyptian scribes
> multiplied by doubling: to multiply by 23 they doubled the other number
> four times and added the rows for 16, 4, 2 and 1, which is writing 23 in
> binary. The Rhind papyrus, copied around 1550 BCE from an older text, records the
> method, and a
> halving-and-doubling form of it is still taught in Ethiopia and Eritrea [n].

**New reference.** Imhausen (2016), as in A2.

**Why.** The section argues that distinction into two is the root of
everything that follows. The oldest surviving arithmetic is built the same
way, which is worth a sentence.

**Wording note.** The Ethiopian method's history is thinly documented, so
the sentence says only that it is taught there, not where or when it began.

### A4. Optional: where the word "algorithm" comes from

**Where.** The first use of "algorithm" in Element 2 (p. 67, "algorithms
that avoid logically irreversible operations").

**Add, as a footnote or a parenthesis.**
> The word comes from al-Khwarizmi, the ninth-century Persian scholar in
> Baghdad whose name was Latinized as *Algoritmi*; his book on equations also
> gave us “algebra”.

**Why.** The book leans on the word throughout. Optional because it is a
credit, not an argument.

## B. Proposed, citation to confirm first

### B1. The second demonstration below threshold

**Where.** Element 21, "Willow’s Breakthrough", p. 432, after "After 30 years,
the threshold had finally been crossed."

**Add.**
> A single result is a claim; a second, independent one is the beginning of
> knowledge. In December 2025 a team at the University of Science and
> Technology of China reported the same threshold crossing on its own
> processor, Zuchongzhi 3.2, with a distance-7 surface code [n]. Its
> suppression factor, about 1.4 for each step up in code size, was smaller
> than Willow’s 2.14, but it was above one, built on different hardware, and
> achieved by a different group. The threshold is no longer one laboratory’s
> result.

**New reference.** *Physical Review Letters*, December 2025, the USTC
Zuchongzhi 3.2 below-threshold paper (Pan, Zhu, Peng and colleagues).
**Confirm the title, volume, article number and DOI against the journal before
this goes in.** Secondary coverage agrees on the result and the date; the
journal page itself could not be reached when this was drafted.

**Optional, same element.** USTC's earlier distance-3 surface code on
Zuchongzhi 2.1: Zhao, Y., et al. (2022), "Realization of an Error-Correcting
Surface Code with Superconducting Qubits." *Physical Review Letters*, 129,
030501.

**Why.** The book's standard is that a result counts when it is reproduced.
Element 21 currently rests on one laboratory. An independent replication on
different hardware is the strongest thing that can be added to it, and it
happens to come from the half of the map the audit found missing.
