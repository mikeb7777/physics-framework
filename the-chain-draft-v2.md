# The Chain

*Draft passage for Rediscovering Oneness, revised 11 October 2026*

Some work is never meant for the person who does it. In the Talmud, a man named Honi sees an old man planting a carob tree and asks how long it will take to bear fruit. Seventy years, the man says. Honi asks whether he expects to live that long. The man answers that he found the world full of carob trees, and as his ancestors planted for him, so he plants for his children (Babylonian Talmud, Ta'anit 23a).

For most of history this was ordinary. Families kept farms and workshops for generations. Tools were handed down to children. A craftsman put his name on what he made and stood behind it for life. Companies were founded to outlast their founders, and the people who ran them thought of themselves as holding the business for whoever came next.

The Swedish navy planted this way on a national scale. Beginning in 1831, it planted oaks on the island of Visingsö, about 300,000 of them, so that future warships would have timber. In 1975, the navy was told the trees were ready. By then ships had long been built of iron and steel, and the navy had no use for them and declined the timber. The forest still stands. The planters' purpose did not survive, but their gift did, and it reached people they could never have pictured.

That habit of mind shaped how people imagined the future. On 14 January 1918, Robert Goddard, the American rocket pioneer, finished a manuscript titled "The Ultimate Migration." In it he imagined that if the sun should one day fail, a ship might carry humanity, or what mattered of it, to another star over a journey of many generations. He never published it. By his own account he kept it in a friend's safe. Later writers took up the idea of the generation ship, and it was a poor one. It asked people who had never chosen the voyage to live out its whole span, and the fiction kept imagining the chain breaking. In Robert Heinlein's *Orphans of the Sky* (1963, assembled from two stories first published in 1941), the crew forgets that they are on a ship at all.

But the idea would not have seemed plausible without the culture around it. A civilization that expects tools, trades and companies to be passed down can imagine a ship that is passed down too. Its flaw was not the thought of generations. It was the assumption that the chain could be kept by a plan alone.

Our own age has largely stopped thinking this way. Work is measured by the quarter, and a life by its own span. Many of us live apart from the families, trades and places that once carried our work forward. That isolation shows us what the older habit was for. A link that cannot see the chain does not stop being part of it. It only forgets, as Heinlein's crew did.

That is worth reclaiming. It does not mean ships, and it does not mean asking our descendants to finish our plans. It means doing our work well enough, and passing it on openly enough, that someone we will never meet can pick it up and carry it further. That is what a planted tree is, and it is also what a book is.
