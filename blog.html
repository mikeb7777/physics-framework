The Veteran's Edge: Peak Performance Through Information-First Principles

Practical Applications for Everyday Life

The principles behind elite veteran performance aren't just for athletes and champions. The same dynamics—crystallized intelligence consuming fluid capacity, the need to maintain adaptive thinking despite accumulating expertise—play out in every aspect of life.

If you're 55+ and your exercise routine has become so automatic you could do it in your sleep, you're experiencing crystallized dominance. If you're switching careers and feeling overwhelmed by how much you "should" already know, you're hitting the expertise paradox. If you're trying to learn something new and it feels harder than it should, accumulated patterns might be crowding out your adaptive capacity.

The good news? The strategies that keep Tom Brady playing at 43 and Diana Nyad swimming at 64 translate directly to everyday challenges. You just have to know what you're actually managing: the balance between useful accumulated knowledge and available mental space for fresh thinking.

Let me show you how this works in practice.

Exercise That Maintains Your Brain (Not Just Your Body)

Barbara is 67 and has been doing the same gym routine for 15 years. Three times a week, same machines, same order, same reps. She can execute it perfectly without thinking, literally on autopilot. That's the problem.

Her body maintains basic fitness (crystallized patterns work fine for that), but her balance has been slowly declining. She trips more easily. Her coordination isn't what it was. And she's bored out of her mind.

What's happening? Her exercise routine has become pure crystallized intelligence. Zero thinking required. Zero adaptation needed. Her brain has completely disengaged from the process.

Meanwhile, her grandson does parkour in the park, climbing, jumping, balancing on uneven surfaces, figuring out novel movement patterns. He's building both physical capability and fluid intelligence simultaneously because every session requires real-time problem-solving.

Barbara doesn't need to do parkour. But she does need to re-engage her fluid intelligence during exercise.

The Movement Vocabulary Approach

Instead of a fixed routine, Barbara now has what I call a movement vocabulary—about 25 different exercises she can execute safely. Push movements like wall push-ups and overhead presses. Pull movements like band rows and doorway pulls. Leg work like squats, lunges, step-ups. Core exercises like planks and bird dogs.

Here's the key: every workout, she improvises. She draws randomly from her vocabulary, maybe push-ups, band rows, squats, and planks today. Maybe incline presses, face pulls, lunges, and dead bugs on Thursday. She decides reps and sets based on how she feels that morning, not a preset plan.

This seems like a small change, but the cognitive impact is enormous. She can't execute on autopilot anymore. Every workout requires decision-making (which movements?), state assessment (how much can I handle today?), and real-time adjustment (that's harder than I expected, modify). She's forced into fluid processing mode.

The physical benefits are obvious—more balanced development, better adaptation, reduced injury risk from repetitive stress. But the cognitive benefits are equally important. Her working memory isn't cluttered with "the routine." Instead, it's engaged with present conditions, making adaptive choices, solving novel combinations.

After three months, Barbara's balance is better than it's been in a decade. She's also lost 12 pounds without trying because she's actually engaged with exercise again rather than mechanically going through motions.

Location as a Fluid Forcing Function

Your environment is either reinforcing crystallized patterns or forcing fluid engagement. If you walk the same route every day, your brain stops processing the environment. You navigate automatically, barely registering where you are. Same gym, same equipment, same spatial cues—all of that triggers automatic execution mode.

Now rotate through different environments weekly. Home workouts using bodyweight. The gym with machines and weights. An outdoor park using benches and playground equipment. A pool for water resistance. Different hiking trails. Different times of day with different light and temperatures.

Each environment eliminates your stored spatial cues. You can't execute automatically because the context keeps changing. Your brain has to process where you are, what's available, how to adapt. This forces fluid engagement.

The research backs this up. Environmental enrichment, exposure to novel, complex environments, demonstrably enhances neuroplasticity in older adults. Your brain physically maintains better capacity for adaptation when regularly challenged with environmental variety.

You're not just exercising your body in different places. You're exercising your fluid intelligence by preventing crystallization into location-dependent routines.

The Playground Revelation

Once a month, Barbara goes to the playground (sometimes with her grandson, sometimes alone). She watches children move and attempts their movements.

Children have almost zero crystallized patterns in movement. They're pure fluid exploration. They crawl, climb, hang, balance, roll, whatever seems interesting, without concern for "proper form" or "correct technique."

Barbara's accumulated expertise has created filters. "You shouldn't do that." "That's not exercise." "That looks silly." But when she notices those thoughts and tries the movement anyway, she discovers capabilities she'd forgotten she had.

Bear crawls engage her whole body in ways machines never did. Hanging from monkey bars reveals shoulder mobility she didn't know she'd lost. Balancing on narrow beams forces real-time adjustments her treadmill walking never required.

The playground exposes her crystallized filters ("adults don't do that") and bypasses them with direct experience. It's external novelty injection applied to movement. She's not trying to become a parkour athlete. She's accessing high-fluid, low-crystallized perspectives to prevent her own movement patterns from rigidifying completely.

Cognitive Health Through Strategic Forgetting

Robert is 71 and terrified of dementia. His father had Alzheimer's, and Robert sees his own memory slipping. He forgets why he walked into a room. He can't remember where he parked. Appointments slip his mind.

His strategy? Try harder to remember everything. Make mental notes. Rehearse information. Keep lists in his head. Use memory as much as possible because "use it or lose it." This is exactly backwards.

Every piece of information Robert tries to hold in his head—the grocery list, his medication schedule, upcoming appointments, his grandson's soccer game times—occupies working memory capacity. That's capacity that could be available for fluid thinking: having conversations, solving problems, adapting to new situations, processing fresh information.

Robert's accumulated all this crystallized intelligence (life knowledge, stored information), and now he's also trying to use his working memory as additional storage. The result? Almost zero capacity left for adaptive thinking. He feels mentally slower not because his brain is failing, but because it's overloaded.

External Memory as Cognitive Liberation

The solution isn't trying harder to remember. It's strategically forgetting, by outsourcing reliable information to external systems. Robert now puts everything in his phone. All appointments in the calendar with alerts. All to-do items in a task app. Shopping lists digital. Medication reminders automatic. Important phone numbers and addresses stored in contacts.

His initial resistance: "But I should be able to remember these things!" The reality: why would you want to? Every piece of information stored externally is working memory freed up for actual thinking. His phone is better at storing schedules than his brain. His brain is better at adaptive problem-solving than his phone. Use each for what it's good at.

After two months of external memory systems, Robert notices something remarkable: he's sharper in conversations. He follows complex discussions better. He adapts to unexpected situations more fluidly. Not because his memory improved, but because he's not wasting working memory on storage tasks.

The "use it or lose it" principle applies to adaptive thinking, not rote storage. Using your working memory as a filing cabinet doesn't make you sharper; it makes you rigid. Freeing that capacity for fluid processing actually maintains cognitive health.

Career Changes: The Expertise Burden

Sarah is 58 and switching from corporate marketing to freelance consulting. She has 30 years of marketing expertise, which should be an advantage. Instead, she feels paralyzed.

Every client conversation triggers her accumulated knowledge. "We tried that in 2003, it didn't work." "That's not how the industry does it." "Best practice says..." Her crystallized intelligence generates constant objections to new approaches.

Meanwhile, her 28-year-old competitor with five years' experience operates with fresh eyes. He suggests unconventional strategies without the burden of knowing all the ways things "should" be done. Clients love his energy and creativity.

Sarah isn't less capable. She's more knowledgeable. But her expertise has become crystallized baggage rather than useful wisdom. Every new situation triggers retrieval of historical patterns rather than fresh analysis. Her working memory is occupied scanning her vast experience library instead of engaging with present reality.

Modular Knowledge Organization

Sarah learns to organize her expertise into modules she can activate selectively rather than having everything constantly active. She creates literal checklists: "Client onboarding essentials" (five key questions, basic frameworks). "Crisis response protocols" (emergency procedures, when needed). "Industry-specific considerations" (activate only for relevant clients).

The key insight: she doesn't need 30 years of accumulated knowledge active for every client conversation. She needs the 5% that's actually relevant to this specific situation. By organizing knowledge into modules and activating them deliberately, she reduces the cognitive load of constant pattern-matching.

Result: when meeting new clients, her working memory is free to actually listen and respond adaptively rather than searching through decades of patterns. She accesses specific expertise when needed rather than maintaining everything at once.

After six months, Sarah's closing more clients than her younger competitor. Not despite her experience, but because she's learned to use it strategically rather than letting it use her.

Learning New Skills: The 62-Year-Old Advantage

Margaret is 62 and learning Spanish, photography, piano, and gardening simultaneously. Her friends think she's crazy. "You're too old to learn all that!" But Margaret understands something they don't: at 62, she has advantages her 25-year-old self didn't.

She knows how she learns. She's completed thousands of learning cycles across her lifetime. She recognizes when she's truly understanding versus just memorizing. She spots her own avoidance patterns. She knows which environments help her focus and which distract her.

This is crystallized intelligence about her own learning process. It's enormously valuable when applied correctly.

The 80/20 Learning Filter

Margaret applies the Pareto principle ruthlessly. What 20% of Spanish vocabulary gives 80% of conversational ability? What 20% of piano techniques enables 80% of the songs she wants to play? What 20% of photography knowledge produces 80% of the results?

The traditional approach: complete conjugations, all tenses, formal grammar, extensive vocabulary. This creates enormous crystallized density before you can have a basic conversation.

Margaret's approach: identify the 20% of knowledge that gives 80% of functionality. For Spanish, that's high-frequency words plus present tense. You can communicate as a functional tourist with surprisingly limited crystallized patterns if they're the right patterns.

She got to "functional tourist" in one month instead of six months of grammar-heavy study. More importantly, she immediately started using the language (fluid engagement through real conversation), which made additional learning easier and more motivating.

The 80/20 filter isn't about being lazy or cutting corners. It's about strategic crystallized accumulation, building the minimum useful patterns first, then adding complexity gradually as capacity allows.

This matches the reality of aging cognitive capacity. You have less working memory available than you did at 25. Trying to learn like a 25-year-old (accumulate everything, process later) doesn't work. Learning like a 62-year-old (accumulate strategically, apply immediately) does.

Learning in Context

Margaret tried learning smartphone photography from online tutorials. Watched hours of videos about aperture, shutter speed, ISO, composition rules, lighting theory. Accumulated lots of crystallized knowledge. Couldn't take a decent photo.

Then she joined a photo walk group. Once a month, they go somewhere interesting and photograph together. She watches people in real situations, asks questions in context, sees immediate results, gets feedback on the spot.

After three photo walks, she'd learned more than months of tutorials taught her. Why? Context provides cues that reduce crystallized retrieval load. When you learn "use rule of thirds" in abstract tutorial, you have to remember it and apply it separately. When you learn it while actually composing a photo, the context itself triggers the pattern. Lower cognitive overhead.

Social learning provides multiple fluid approaches. Different people solve the same compositional challenge differently. Margaret sees various strategies (fluid exposure) rather than memorizing one "correct" approach (crystallized rigidity).

Immediate application forces fluid processing. You can't theorize about photography while actually photographing. You have to make real-time decisions with imperfect information. That's fluid intelligence in action.

She applied this insight to cooking too. Instead of studying recipes and techniques at home (decontextualized crystallized accumulation), she takes in-person cooking classes where she actually prepares food alongside an instructor in real time (contextualized fluid engagement).

The learning sticks better, applies more naturally, and feels less overwhelming. Same cognitive capacity limitations, different approach, dramatically different outcomes.

"Good Enough" Goals

Margaret's tried learning four instruments: piano, guitar, ukulele, and harmonica. If her goal for each had been "master the instrument," she'd have quit all four within months (too overwhelming, too much crystallized density required).

Instead, each had a specific "good enough" goal. Piano: Play ten songs she loves well enough to enjoy playing them. Not performance quality, not technically perfect, just "good enough for me." Guitar: Play campfire songs with her grandkids. Three chords, simple strumming, singalong quality. Ukulele: Accompany herself singing. Basic chords, simple patterns, functional musicality. Harmonica: Blues harmonica for personal enjoyment. One position, basic technique, expressive playing.

These goals are achievable crystallized scopes. She's not accumulating "complete instrument mastery" (crushing density). She's accumulating "specific functional capability" (manageable density).

More importantly, she reached "good enough" on each instrument within 3-6 months. She's been using all four for years now, continuously improving but never feeling the pressure to master them. The "good enough" frame keeps crystallized accumulation manageable while maintaining fluid enjoyment.

The irony: by not trying to master anything, she's sustained engagement with everything. By keeping crystallized density manageable, she's maintained fluid capacity across multiple domains.

Energy-Matched Learning

Margaret discovered her fluid capacity varies dramatically by time of day. She tracked for two weeks: every two hours, she rated her energy level, focus quality, and how well learning went if she tried. The pattern was clear—mornings were her high-fluid window, afternoons were medium, evenings were depleted.

Now she matches learning intensity to capacity. New concepts and difficult practice happen in the morning when fluid capacity is highest. Review and enjoyable application happen in afternoons when she has medium capacity. Passive exposure (watching, listening) or rest happens in evenings when she's depleted.

This single change doubled her learning efficiency. She's not trying harder or spending more time. She's matching cognitive demands to available capacity.

Most people learn when convenient—evenings after work, weekends when they have time. But that's often when fluid capacity is lowest. They're trying to do high-fluid-demand activities (learning novel skills) with minimal fluid capacity available. No wonder it feels hard.

Margaret's energy-matched approach respects her biological reality. She has less total fluid capacity than she did at 25 (biological aging), but she has more control over when she uses it (life flexibility). By optimizing timing, she compensates for reduced capacity.

The Pattern Across Domains

Whether it's exercise, cognitive health, career changes, or learning new skills, the same principles apply.

Manage crystallized accumulation: Keep patterns modular and organized. Prune outdated ones. Focus on the 20% that gives 80% of functionality. Offload what can be external. This prevents your accumulated knowledge from consuming all your working memory.

Force fluid engagement: Novel environments where patterns don't apply. Improvisation that requires synthesis. Beginner perspectives that bypass expert filters. Real contexts that demand adaptation. This maintains your capacity for adaptive thinking.

Optimize capacity: Learn when fluid intelligence is highest. Rest to restore cognitive resources. Set achievable goals that don't overwhelm. Match demands to available capacity. This respects your biological limits while maximizing what's possible.

The result isn't performing like you did at 25. The result is performing optimally for who you are now—maintaining fluid thinking despite crystallized accumulation, expertise without rigidity, growth without overwhelm.

That's the veteran's edge. Not superhuman ability, but strategic management of the fluid-crystallized trade-off. And it's available to anyone willing to approach learning, performance, and development with this understanding in mind.

Discussion By Michael K. Baines December 15, 2025

What Is an Interstellar Object, Really?

When 3I/ATLAS swept through our solar system this year, headlines announced the arrival of our third "interstellar comet." The James Webb Space Telescope captured spectra showing carbon dioxide, water ice, and a telltale coma of outgassing material. It behaves like a comet, so we call it one.

But should we?

In the 18th century, the French Academy of Sciences—then the highest scientific authority in the world—declared that stones could not fall from the sky. The theoretical framework demanded empty space between celestial bodies; therefore, eyewitness accounts had to be wrong. Museum curators across Europe discarded genuine meteorites as shameful relics of superstition. It took a rain of three thousand stones at L'Aigle in 1803 to finally overturn this consensus.

We might be making the same mistake with interstellar visitors—not by denying their existence, but by assuming we already know what they are.

The Problem with "Comet"

The moment we label something, we import assumptions. "Comet" carries with it an entire framework: a pristine chunk of primordial ice from a protoplanetary disk, stored cold for billions of years, now activated by stellar heating. The research questions naturally follow: How does this interstellar comet compare to our comets? What does it tell us about comet formation elsewhere?

These may be the wrong questions entirely.

When astronomers first detected quasars in the 1950s, they looked like stars through optical telescopes. So scientists called them "quasi-stellar objects," star-like things. When spectra revealed emission lines that matched no known element, the extragalactic explanation was rejected because it implied luminosities that seemed impossibly high. The name itself—"quasi-stellar"—embedded an assumption that constrained thinking for years. They weren't stars at all. They were supermassive black holes at the centers of distant galaxies, phenomena so far outside existing categories that the framework had to be rebuilt from scratch.

"Interstellar comet" may be our generation's "quasi-stellar object," a name that captures superficial behavior while obscuring the actual nature of what we're observing.

What We Actually Know

We have detected exactly three interstellar objects. Three. From that vanishingly small sample, we've already sorted them into existing bins. 'Oumuamua was strange enough that serious scientists proposed it might be a spacecraft—an intellectually lazy hypothesis that explains anomalies by invoking the maximally unconstrained explanation. 2I/Borisov looked sufficiently comet-like that it raised fewer eyebrows. Now 3I/ATLAS, with its dramatic CO₂ outgassing, gets filed under "comet, interstellar variety."

But consider what our own solar system produces: rocky worlds, icy moons, differentiated planetesimals, and everything in between. Why should interstellar space deliver only one category of object?

Current estimates suggest there may be trillions of rogue planets wandering our galaxy—potentially outnumbering stars by a factor of twenty or more. These aren't just failed Jupiter-like gas giants. Simulations indicate that typical ejected planets have median masses around 0.8 Earth masses. Rocky worlds, cast out from their parent systems by gravitational chaos, planetary collisions, or stellar death.

These objects collide. They fragment. They erode. Over hundreds of millions or billions of years, the debris spreads through interstellar space, mixing with material ejected from countless other systems.

So why do we keep detecting things that look like comets? The answer is almost certainly observational bias. Comets announce themselves. When an icy body approaches a star, it outgasses, producing a coma and tail visible across enormous distances. A dark, rocky fragment from a shattered exoplanet would be essentially invisible unless it passed very close to us. We see what glows.

The Palimpsest Problem

Here's what makes interstellar objects genuinely fascinating, far more fascinating than comfortable categorization allows.

Recent analysis of 3I/ATLAS suggests that its anomalous CO₂-to-water ratio results from galactic cosmic ray processing during its interstellar transit. The radiation converted CO to CO₂ while building up an organic-rich crust. Critically, current outgassing appears to sample only this processed outer layer, roughly the top 15-20 meters. We may not be seeing the object's original composition at all.

Think about what that means. The object we observe is a palimpsest, layers of modification written over whatever it originally was. During millions or billions of years of interstellar travel, it experienced cosmic ray bombardment transforming surface chemistry, passage through molecular clouds with their own gases and dust, potential accretion of interstellar ice onto its surface, gravitational encounters that could fracture or spin the body, possible capture and re-ejection by other stellar systems, and varying radiation environments depending on galactic position.

The surface we're measuring may bear little relationship to the interior. And the interior may bear little relationship to the object's form when it was first ejected from its home system.

A chunk of an icy moon ejected by a giant impact would outgas when it approached our Sun. A fragment of a water-rich super-Earth's mantle would outgas. Material from a shattered Pluto-analog that subsequently accumulated interstellar volatiles during its journey would outgas. "Comet-like behavior" tells us about current activity, not origin or nature.

Learning to See Again

When Chiron was discovered in 1977, astronomers classified it as an asteroid. Twelve years later, it developed a coma—the signature behavior of comets. But it was far too large to be a typical comet nucleus, and its orbit didn't fit cometary patterns. Today Chiron carries dual designations: minor planet 2060 Chiron and comet 95P/Chiron. It also has rings. Its existence forced the creation of an entirely new category—Centaurs—because reality refused to fit the available boxes.

We had to let Chiron be what it was before we could understand it.

The first astronomers to study comets didn't know what they were. They observed, measured, catalogued. Over centuries, patterns emerged. Theory developed from data, not the reverse.

We should extend the same courtesy to interstellar visitors. They are what they are. The outgassing of 3I/ATLAS tells us about its current behavior, not necessarily its origin or nature. Instead of asking "How does this interstellar comet compare to our comets?", we might ask: What is its density and internal structure? A homogeneous icy body would differ dramatically from a differentiated fragment of a larger world. What do the isotope ratios reveal? Different stellar populations produce different nucleosynthetic signatures. Carbon-12 to carbon-13 ratios, oxygen isotopes, and trace elements could fingerprint which type of star system produced this material. Are there refractory inclusions? Rocky or metalite materials that survived surface processing would tell us about the object's deeper history. Can we distinguish accreted material from original composition? The interstellar medium isn't empty. Material gathered during transit might form distinct layers.

A fragment of a shattered exoplanet, chemically weathered by its passage through the galaxy, potentially carrying stratified layers recording different epochs of its journey—that's not a comet. It's a geological core sample of another solar system and of interstellar space itself. It's a time capsule containing information about planetary formation, destruction, and galactic chemistry that we cannot obtain any other way.

The Invitation

With only three detected objects, we have no statistical basis for understanding what interstellar space actually contains. We're like naturalists who've seen three birds fly past the window and concluded that all animals have feathers. The Roman Space Telescope, launching in 2027, should dramatically increase our detection rate. Perhaps then patterns will emerge. Perhaps we'll discover categories we never imagined.

Until then, the honest answer is: we don't know what these objects are. And that uncertainty is not a failure—it's an invitation. Every assumption we set aside creates room for discovery. Every familiar category we refuse to impose opens the possibility of finding something genuinely new.

The universe has been sending us messages for billions of years, written in rock and ice and cosmic ray damage. We're only just learning to read them. Let's not pretend we already know what they say.
