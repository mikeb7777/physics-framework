# Version 6G: proposed changes

**Status: applied to the Word file, 13 October 2026** (Michael: "let's do 6G
now"). `A Quest for The Big TOE Version 6G.docx` is built from 6F by
`tools/book_6g_2026_10_13.py`. A1 to A5, B1 and B2 are in, and the edition line
reads "Version 6G". The A4 echo reference points to Appendix Element 19,
Section F, where the website appendix sets out the echo prediction. **Waiting on
the PDF**, exported from Word; the download page switches once every change is
checked in its text. The website was corrected first (framework page, glossary,
appendix).

**Why.** Michael asked whether the physics and mathematics definitions of a
singularity differ, and whether the book states them accurately. They do differ,
and the review found five places in Element 19 where the book says more than
general relativity does. None of them changes the framework's argument. Each one
removes a sentence a physicist reviewer would challenge first, and two of them
make the argument stronger.

**The background, in short.**

- In mathematics, a singularity is a point where a function is undefined or
  stops behaving smoothly (1/x at x = 0). It is still a point of the space, and
  it is studied through limits.
- In general relativity, a spacetime is singular when the paths of particles or
  light end after a finite time and cannot be continued (geodesic
  incompleteness). The singularity is not a point in spacetime; it is where
  spacetime stops.
- The Penrose-Hawking theorems prove geodesic incompleteness. They do not prove
  infinite density or curvature, and they do not say whether the end is a moment
  or a place.
- Inside a non-rotating (Schwarzschild) black hole the singularity is spacelike:
  a moment. Inside a rotating (Kerr) black hole it is a timelike ring: in
  principle avoidable. Real black holes rotate.

## A. Corrections

### A1. What the singularity theorems prove

**Where.** Element 19, "The Singularity That Isn't", first paragraph.

**Now.**
> General relativity predicts that gravitational collapse produces a singularity:
> a point of infinite density where the equations break down entirely. This
> prediction emerges from the Penrose-Hawking singularity theorems [20], which
> prove that under certain reasonable conditions, singularities are
> mathematically unavoidable.

**Change to.**
> General relativity predicts that gravitational collapse produces a singularity.
> In the classical solutions, density and curvature grow without limit as the
> centre is approached and the equations break down. The Penrose-Hawking
> singularity theorems [20] prove that under certain reasonable conditions this
> cannot be avoided, in a precise sense: the paths of falling particles and of
> light end after a finite time and cannot be continued. The theorems do not
> themselves prove that anything becomes infinite.

**Why.** "A point of infinite density" is the popular gloss, not what the
theorems prove. The rest of the paragraph ("those theorems assume classical
physics all the way down…") stands as written.

### A2. Not every black hole singularity is a moment

**Where.** Element 19, "Open Questions from an Information-First Perspective",
the paragraph beginning "The Penrose-Hawking singularity theorems establish that the singularity at the origin of the universe is a
temporal boundary".

**Now.**
> The Penrose-Hawking singularity theorems establish that the singularity at the
> origin of the universe is a temporal boundary: not a location in space but the
> boundary of time itself. The same theorems establish that the singularity
> inside every black hole is also a temporal boundary.

**Change to.**
> In the standard cosmological solutions, the singularity at the origin of the
> universe is a temporal boundary: not a location in space but the boundary of
> time itself. Inside a non-rotating black hole, the singularity is a temporal
> boundary in the same sense. The singularity theorems put both in the same
> class, because in both the paths of particles and light come to an end; they do
> not decide whether that end is a moment or a place. Real black holes rotate,
> and in the rotating solution the classical singularity is a ring that some
> paths can in principle avoid.

**Why.** As written, the paragraph contradicts the book's own Kerr section
("What Asymmetry Does to the Equations"), which correctly says rotation turns the
point into a ring. The rotating case actually supports the framework's argument
that asymmetry changes the endpoint.

### A3. The infalling observer

**Where.** Element 19, the next paragraph, beginning "Inside the event horizon,
the direction toward the singularity becomes timelike."

**Now.**
> Inside the event horizon, the direction toward the singularity becomes
> timelike. It is not a place you reach. It is a moment you cannot avoid. The
> infalling observer experiences no breakdown, no discontinuity, no local signal
> that anything unusual is happening. The physics continues. What ends is the
> classical description.

**Change to.**
> Inside the event horizon of a non-rotating black hole, the direction toward the
> singularity becomes timelike. It is not a place you reach. It is a moment you
> cannot avoid. At the horizon itself, the infalling observer experiences no
> breakdown, no discontinuity, no local signal that anything unusual is
> happening. Closer in, tidal forces grow without limit, and that is where the
> classical description ends. What the framework questions is whether the
> physics ends with it.

**Why.** "No local signal" is true at the horizon and false near the classical
singularity, where tidal stretching diverges.

### A4. "The universe as evidence"

**Where.** Element 19, "Chaos as Information Processing", the paragraph beginning
"The universe as evidence".

**Now.**
> If true singularities formed inside them, singularities that represent literal
> endpoints of spacetime where causality breaks down, that breakdown would
> propagate. The universe continues. Structure persists. We are here to discuss
> black holes. This is not an anthropic tautology. It is a data point. The
> universe has been stress-tested by its most extreme objects and passed.

**Change to.**
> If true singularities formed inside them, they would be literal endpoints of
> spacetime. In classical general relativity, though, each one would sit behind
> its event horizon and could affect nothing outside. So the universe continuing
> is not, by itself, evidence against singularities. The evidence has to come
> from the black holes themselves. If the interior is a quantum structure rather
> than a singularity, faint gravitational-wave echoes should follow the main
> signal when black holes merge (Appendix, Element 19). That is the test the
> framework commits to.

**Why.** The original is the claim a referee would reject first. The
replacement points to the book's own falsifiable test instead.

### A5. The uncertainty argument is heuristic

**Where.** Element 19, "Chaos as Information Processing", the paragraph
beginning "The uncertainty principle makes this definitive [21]."

**Now (first and last sentences).**
> The uncertainty principle makes this definitive [21]. … The uncertainty
> principle does not merely make singularities hard to achieve. It generates the
> precise resistance that prevents them.

**Change to.**
> The uncertainty principle points the same way [21]. … On the framework's
> reading, the uncertainty principle does not merely make singularities hard to
> achieve; it generates the resistance that prevents them. This is a heuristic
> argument, not a calculation. A full answer needs a quantum theory of gravity.

**Why.** House style: "consistent with", never "confirmed". The argument is
suggestive, and the book should say so.

## B. Additions

### B1. A short definition box

**Where.** Element 19, "The Singularity That Isn't", after A1's paragraph.

**Add.**
> **Two meanings of one word.** In mathematics, a singularity is a point where a
> function is undefined or stops behaving smoothly, such as 1/x at x = 0. The
> point is still part of the number line, and everything about it is learned
> from limits: how the function behaves as the point is approached. In general
> relativity, a singularity is not a point in spacetime at all. It is where
> spacetime stops: where the paths of particles and light end and cannot be
> continued. Physicists also separate coordinate singularities, which disappear
> in better coordinates (the event horizon is one), from curvature
> singularities, which do not. When this book says the singularity is a
> mathematical fixed point rather than a physical state, it means the first
> sense is being mistaken for the second.

### B2. The relational point

**Where.** Element 19, at the end of "How Not to Have a Universe", after "Neither
can be a starting point for anything, because neither exists."

**Add.**
> There is a further point. Everything general relativity can say about a black
> hole, it says from outside, through relationships: its mass by how things orbit
> it, its spin by how it drags spacetime around it, its charge by its field. At a
> singularity there is nothing left to relate to, and nothing is defined. The
> absence of true singularities would be consistent with relationship being more
> fundamental than the things related. It would not prove it: other approaches to
> quantum gravity also remove singularities, and none has yet been observed or
> ruled out.

## C. Not proposed

- **The isoperimetric parallel and the BKL section.** Accurate as written.
- **The Kerr point-to-ring section.** Accurate. A2 brings the temporal-boundary
  paragraph into line with it.
- **The Introduction's "infinite density in zero volume" (Preface).** It is
  framed as what cosmology "is said" to claim, and it is followed by "conditions
  where our physics admittedly breaks down". It can stay.
